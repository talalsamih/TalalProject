## Executive Summary

This research explores two related philosophical positions that challenge conventional materialist approaches to understanding reality: panpsychism (the view that consciousness is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of nature) and pancomputationalism (the view that all physical systems perform computations). Both theories propose that what we typically consider emergent or complex properties—consciousness and computation—may in fact be fundamental features of the physical world. This document synthesizes contemporary philosophical and scientific literature to examine how these frameworks might offer new approaches to understanding the nature of mind, matter, and reality itself.

## 1. Panpsychism: Consciousness as a Fundamental Property

### 1.1 Core Commitments

Panpsychism represents a radical departure from standard physicalist approaches to consciousness. In contemporary philosophical debates, panpsychism is generally understood as the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world. More specifically, the dominant form discussed today is panexperientialism—the view that conscious experience is fundamental and ubiquitous throughout nature.

The basic commitment of panpsychism, as articulated by leading contemporary proponents, is that the fundamental constituents of reality—perhaps electrons and quarks—have incredibly simple forms of experience. For the panpsychist, consciousness exists on a continuum that extends far beyond biological organisms, with fundamental physical entities possessing extremely rudimentary forms of consciousness that reflect their extremely simple nature.

This view challenges the conventional assumption that consciousness emerges only at certain levels of complexity. Instead, panpsychists propose that simple forms of experience are woven into the fabric of reality at its most basic level, and that the rich, complex consciousness we experience as humans results from the combination of these simpler forms.

### 1.2 Historical Context and Contemporary Revival

While panpsychism has ancient roots in various philosophical traditions, it fell out of favor during the rise of mechanistic materialism in the 17th century and remained largely dormant in Western philosophy for centuries. However, towards the end of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, the continuing failure of physicalists to come up with a satisfying account of consciousness has led many to look for alternatives.

As a result, a significant and growing minority of analytic philosophers have begun seriously to explore the potential of panpsychism. There is growing dissatisfaction with the physicalist approaches to consciousness which dominated the late 20th century, and in this climate panpsychism is increasingly being taken up as a serious option in mainstream philosophy of mind.

### 1.3 Russellian Monism: The Intrinsic Nature Argument

Much of contemporary panpsychism is grounded in Russellian monism, an approach to the mind-body problem proposed by Bertrand Russell in his 1927 book "The Analysis of Matter," with Arthur Eddington independently expressing similar thoughts in his Gifford lectures of the same year. This view has recently been revived as a "Russell-inspired" approach to consciousness.

The motivation for Russellian monism emerges from what philosophers call the "intrinsic nature problem." Physics appears to only tell us about the relational or dispositional structure of the physical world. Fundamental physical properties such as spin, mass, and charge are exhaustively characterized in terms of their relations to other fundamental properties. However, there is reason to think that relations need relata with intrinsic properties, and dispositions need categorical grounds.

Russellian monists argue that we should consider the fact that mental properties are intrinsic and categorical, and are in fact the only such properties we know with certainty to exist. They ask whether mental properties could be the intrinsic ground of everything physical—this is what Russellian monist panpsychism affirms. On this view, consciousness is not an add-on to physical reality, but rather constitutes the intrinsic nature of what physics describes only structurally.

Some Russellian monists think that the intrinsic nature of fundamental matter is itself consciousness-involving; others propose that it involves non-phenomenal "protophenomenal" properties that can collectively constitute phenomenal properties, resulting in a variety known as Russellian panprotopsychism.

## 2. Philip Goff and Contemporary Panpsychism

### 2.1 Goff's Contribution

Philip Goff, a British philosopher and professor at Durham University, has become one of the most prominent contemporary advocates for panpsychism. His research focuses on philosophy of mind and consciousness, and he advocates what he calls a "third way"—a version of Russellian monism that attempts to account for reality's intrinsic nature by positing that consciousness is a fundamental, ubiquitous feature of the physical world.

### 2.2 Galileo's Error

Goff's 2019 book "Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Consciousness" argues that modern science made a crucial mistake at its inception. Goff's case is rooted in an analysis of the philosophical underpinnings of modern science and based on the early twentieth-century work of Arthur Eddington and Bertrand Russell.

The book's central argument is that Galileo, in establishing the mathematical foundations of physics, removed consciousness and qualitative properties from the domain of scientific investigation, focusing only on quantitative properties that could be measured mathematically. According to Goff, this move created an artificial divide that has made the problem of consciousness seemingly intractable for physical science.

His critique of materialism is based on thought experiments that aim to demonstrate that objective knowledge cannot be extended to encapsulate subjective experience, and therefore consciousness cannot be explained in terms of the physical brain alone. A panpsychist solution proposes that the intrinsic nature of physical reality is consciousness itself.

### 2.3 The Hard Problem of Consciousness

Goff's work, along with that of other contemporary panpsychists, addresses what philosopher David Chalmers famously termed "the hard problem of consciousness." The terms "hard problem" and "easy problems" were coined by Chalmers in a 1994 talk at The Science of Consciousness conference in Tucson, Arizona.

The hard problem is the problem of explaining the relationship between physical phenomena, such as brain processes, and experience—that is, phenomenal consciousness, or mental states and events with phenomenal qualities or qualia. The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience itself. As Thomas Nagel put it in his influential 1974 paper, there is "something it is like" to be a conscious organism.

The challenge arises because it does not seem that the qualitative and subjective aspects of conscious experience—how consciousness "feels" and the fact that it is directly "for me"—fit into a physicalist ontology. There seems to be an unbridgeable explanatory gap between the physical world and consciousness.

Panpsychism attempts to bridge this gap not by explaining consciousness in terms of non-conscious physical processes, but by positing that consciousness is already present at the fundamental level. Rather than trying to explain the emergence of consciousness from non-conscious matter, panpsychism tries to explain the complex consciousness of humans and other animals in terms of simpler forms of consciousness which are postulated to exist in simpler forms of matter.

## 3. The Combination Problem in Panpsychism

### 3.1 Defining the Problem

While panpsychism may offer a solution to the hard problem of consciousness by making consciousness fundamental, it faces its own significant challenge: the combination problem. This problem roughly asks: how do the experiences of fundamental physical entities such as quarks and photons combine to yield the familiar sort of human conscious experience?

The combination problem can be traced to William James, but was given its present name by William Seager in 1995. James's objections against a version of panpsychism he labeled the "mind dust" theory are the inspiration for the so-called combination problem.

### 3.2 The Challenge of Mental Combination

Contemporary philosophers have identified something specifically troubling about the notion of mental combination, a concern that doesn't obviously arise in the physical case. While we can easily understand how physical particles combine to form larger physical systems, the idea of many minds forming some other mind is much harder to conceptualize.

The kind of mental combination which is generally taken to be most troubling is what philosophers call "subject-summing": the combination of distinct conscious subjects into a single conscious mind. How do the individual experiences of billions of particles combine to create the unified experience of a single human consciousness?

This raises several perplexing questions:
- Do the micro-experiences of particles get preserved in the macro-experience, or do they disappear?
- How does a new unified subject emerge from many separate subjects?
- Why don't we experience the individual consciousnesses of the particles that make us up?
- What determines which collections of conscious particles form unified subjects and which don't?

### 3.3 Current Status

The general consensus among panpsychists is that there is currently no wholly adequate solution to the combination problem. Much of the debate concerning the viability of panpsychism centers on this problem. However, proponents argue that this is not obviously a reason to think the panpsychist research project is not worth pursuing. They point out that all theories of consciousness face difficult problems, and that the combination problem may ultimately prove more tractable than the hard problem faced by physicalism.

Some panpsychists have proposed that the combination problem reveals not a flaw in panpsychism but rather limitations in our current conceptual frameworks for understanding the nature of subjects and experience. Others are actively working on technical solutions involving various forms of "emergence" or new ways of understanding how conscious subjects relate to their constituents.

## 4. Pancomputationalism: Everything Computes

### 4.1 Core Thesis

Pancomputationalism represents a parallel claim to panpsychism, but in the domain of computation rather than consciousness. The core thesis is that all physical systems—atoms, rocks, hurricanes, and toasters—perform computations. This view seems to be increasingly popular among some philosophers and physicists, though it remains highly controversial.

### 4.2 Varieties of Pancomputationalism

Research reveals several distinct varieties of pancomputationalism:

**Unlimited Pancomputationalism:** This version holds that literally every physical system implements every possible computation. Arguments for this position go back to philosophical thought experiments like Hinckfuss's pail, which was proposed as a counterexample to computational functionalism.

**Ontic Pancomputationalism:** Unlike philosophical versions that originate in debates about functionalism, ontic pancomputationalism originates in physics itself. Some physicists and authors argue that the physical universe is fundamentally computational—that the universe itself is a computing system, and everything in it is a computing system too. This is not merely a useful metaphor but a claim about the basic ontology of reality.

**Digital Physics:** According to this approach, championed by some physicists, the physical world is computational at its most fundamental level. This includes the "it from bit" view associated with physicist John Archibald Wheeler, which suggests that information is more fundamental than matter or energy.

### 4.3 Historical Development: Zuse and Fredkin

The foundations of modern digital physics were laid by two pioneering figures:

**Konrad Zuse:** Zuse pioneered digital physics in his 1969 book "Calculating Space" (Rechnender Raum). The idea suggests that space and time are quantized on a discrete grid, with all phenomena arising from simple, deterministic rules applied to binary states, much like a cellular automaton where stable patterns represent particles and waves. Juergen Schmidhuber makes the case that Konrad Zuse had articulated this idea as early as 1967.

**Edward Fredkin:** Fredkin, who coined the term "digital physics" in 1978, expanded this into digital mechanics, proposing the universe as a reversible universal cellular automaton (RUCA) that conserves information—treating bits as fundamental units analogous to energy or mass, ensuring no information is created or destroyed in physical processes.

### 4.4 Contemporary Development

Later thinkers like Stephen Wolfram built upon this foundation, exploring how simple computational rules can generate complex patterns and arguing for a fundamentally digital universe. The two main computational formalisms considered are cellular automata (classical) and quantum computing (non-classical).

### 4.5 Critical Perspectives

Critics argue that the force of pancomputationalist claims is diminished when weaknesses in their supporting computational descriptions are exposed. The most sensational claims may be unwarranted, whereas modest claims offer little more than recognition of causal similarities between physical systems and formal computational systems. The challenge is determining whether physical systems genuinely compute, or whether computation is merely a useful metaphor or descriptive framework we impose on nature.

## 5. Universal Computation and Consciousness: Potential Connections

### 5.1 Integrated Information Theory

One framework that attempts to link consciousness and computation is Integrated Information Theory (IIT), proposed by neuroscientist Giulio Tononi in 2004. IIT proposes a mathematical model for the consciousness of a system, intended to explain why some physical systems (such as human brains) are conscious, and to be capable of providing a concrete inference about whether any physical system is conscious, to what degree, and what particular experience it has.

According to IIT, integrated information (Φ, or "phi") corresponds to the quantity of consciousness. The theory claims that consciousness is identical to a certain kind of information, the realization of which requires physical, not merely functional, integration, and which can be measured mathematically according to the phi metric.

Rather than try to start from physical principles and arrive at consciousness, IIT "starts with consciousness" (accepts the existence of our own consciousness as certain) and reasons about the properties that a postulated physical substrate would need to have in order to account for it.

### 5.2 Computational Challenges and Criticisms

According to physicist Max Tegmark, "the integration measure proposed by IIT is computationally infeasible to evaluate for large systems, growing super-exponentially with the system's information content." This makes it currently practical only for idealized systems with a few units, not for realistic systems like human brains.

IIT 4.0, released recently, incorporates several developments of the past ten years, including a more accurate formulation of the axioms as postulates and mathematical expressions, and an explicit assessment of causal relations. However, despite significant interest, IIT remains controversial. In 2023, a number of scholars characterized it as unfalsifiable pseudoscience for lacking sufficient empirical support, a claim reiterated in a 2025 Nature Neuroscience commentary.

### 5.3 Convergence Points

Despite their different starting points, panpsychism and pancomputationalism share several interesting features:

1. **Fundamentality:** Both propose that properties typically considered emergent or high-level (consciousness and computation) are actually fundamental features of reality.

2. **Ubiquity:** Both suggest these properties are ubiquitous, present throughout nature rather than appearing only in special circumstances.

3. **Scalar Nature:** Both allow for degrees or gradations—just as panpsychism posits simple experiences in simple systems and complex experiences in complex systems, pancomputationalism can accommodate the idea that different systems perform computations of varying complexity.

4. **Challenge to Emergence:** Both theories challenge standard emergentist views that consciousness and computation are properties that only emerge at certain levels of complexity.

## 6. Consciousness in All Matter: Implications and Future Directions

### 6.1 Philosophical Implications

If panpsychism is correct, it would revolutionize our understanding of the place of mind in nature. Rather than seeing consciousness as a rare and mysterious phenomenon confined to certain biological organisms, we would understand it as woven into the fabric of reality itself. This would dissolve the hard problem of consciousness by denying its central premise—that consciousness must be explained as emerging from wholly non-conscious matter.

Similarly, if pancomputationalism is correct, it would transform our understanding of the relationship between information, computation, and physical reality. Rather than seeing computation as something that physical systems can be used to implement, we would understand computation as describing what physical systems fundamentally are.

### 6.2 Scientific Implications

Both theories raise profound questions for empirical science:

- How can we test whether fundamental particles possess rudimentary forms of consciousness?
- What experimental evidence could distinguish between genuine computation and mere physical causation?
- Does consciousness correlate with information integration, as IIT suggests?
- Are there measurable differences between physical systems that do and don't possess consciousness?

### 6.3 The Synthesis Question

Perhaps the most intriguing possibility is that consciousness and computation are deeply related or even identical. If consciousness is what information integration feels like "from the inside," and if all physical systems integrate information to some degree, then panpsychism and pancomputationalism might be two perspectives on the same underlying reality.

This would suggest that the universe is not only fundamentally conscious but fundamentally computational, with these being two aspects of a single underlying nature. The "hardware" of the universe would be its physical structure, the "software" would be the computations it performs, and consciousness would be what it's like to be such a computational system from the inside.

### 6.4 Outstanding Challenges

Both theories face significant challenges that must be addressed:

**For Panpsychism:**
- The combination problem remains without a fully satisfactory solution
- The theory must explain why consciousness seems to disappear under anesthesia or in certain brain states
- It must provide some empirical predictions or ways to test the theory
- It needs to explain the apparent unity of consciousness despite the plurality of underlying conscious elements

**For Pancomputationalism:**
- The theory must provide clear criteria for what counts as computation
- It must avoid the trivialization objection (that if everything computes, the claim becomes vacuous)
- It needs to explain what makes some computations special or relevant (e.g., why brain computations produce consciousness while rock computations don't)
- It must address whether computation is an intrinsic physical property or an observer-relative description

### 6.5 Current Research Trajectories

Contemporary research continues to explore these ideas:

- Philosophers are developing more sophisticated accounts of mental combination
- Physicists are investigating whether the universe can be modeled as a quantum computer
- Neuroscientists are testing theories like IIT that attempt to bridge consciousness and information
- Computer scientists are exploring the nature of computation and whether there are fundamental limits to what can compute

### 6.6 Conclusion

Panpsychism and pancomputationalism represent bold attempts to reconceptualize our understanding of consciousness, computation, and the nature of physical reality. While both face significant challenges, they offer genuine alternatives to conventional materialist approaches that have struggled to account for consciousness. Whether these theories are correct remains an open question, but their exploration pushes philosophy and science to think more deeply about the fundamental nature of mind, matter, and reality itself.

The possibility that consciousness and computation are universal features of nature—perhaps two aspects of the same underlying reality—remains one of the most fascinating and important questions in contemporary philosophy of mind and metaphysics. As our scientific understanding continues to advance and our philosophical frameworks become more sophisticated, we may find ourselves moving closer to answering these profound questions about the nature of existence itself.
